Salary Equity and DEI Analysis

1. Context and Business Question

In this project, we analyze an employee dataset to answer a critical question in organizations: Is there a gender pay gap unexplained by factors such as experience, education, or department? In other words, we are looking to identify whether there is a gap unexplained by the factors that commonly influence salary levels in a company. As HR Professionals, I am sure my company measures people’s capabilities by their hard and soft skills, and not by their gender? We would all love to live in this perfect world. But the reality is that biases exist, and discovering and limiting them is our job.

More than a simple analysis of the provided data, I seek to build a model that allows us to isolate the effect of gender on salary, controlling for other truly relevant variables. I invite you to reflect: It is already difficult enough to be a woman in Mexico, without being questioned simply because of how you were born.

2. Data Loading and Initial Diagnosis

First, we load the dataset and perform an EDA (Exploratory Data Analysis). Here we will identify the state of the dataset, what problems it has, and how we can solve them to make optimal use of it. In an ideal world, datasets generated by questionnaires, surveys, etc., are perfect. But since humans are not perfect, our responses and the tools we design to measure them will also have errors that we do not anticipate. We will focus on finding null values, format inconsistencies (date, time, etc.), and of course, identifying outliers.

3. Data Cleaning and Preparation

Before conducting any analysis, we need to clean the dataset and prepare the data. This step is probably the most important one.

By deeply understanding the quality of the dataset or the data we’re working with, we can perform better analysis, use more efficient methods, and choose algorithms that make sense.

If our data isn’t properly prepared, the insights won’t be reliable. There will be unexplained errors and biases, or simply everything will be wrong. It’s crucial to have good judgment and know how to handle cleaning and preparation, so we can make the best use of the data we have available. We can’t perform miracles, but we can certainly reduce errors.

Code 
# 1. Cleaning of categorical features (eg: 'Department' o 'Job Title')
# Ajusta 'Department' al nombre real de la columna de departamento en tu dataset
columnas_categoria = ['Department', 'Gender', 'Status', 'Location'] 
for col in columnas_categoria:
    if col in df.columns:
        df[col] = df[col].astype(str).str.strip().str.title()

# 2. Handling of Missing values
# Numerical:  we will be imputing median since it's more resilient towards outliers.
for col in df.columns:
    if df[col].isnull().any():
        if pd.api.types.is_numeric_dtype(df[col]):
            df[col] = df[col].fillna(df[col].median())
        else:
            df[col] = df[col].fillna('No Especificado')


# 3. Feature engineering: Create 'Tenure_Years'
# Update 'Date of Joining' or 'Joining Date' to the actual name of the date column in your dataset
if 'Date of Joining' in df.columns:
    df['Joining Date'] = pd.to_datetime(df['Date of Joining'], errors='coerce')
    df['Antiguedad_Años'] = (pd.Timestamp.now() - df['Joining Date']).dt.days / 365.25

Summary of Changes

The following transformations were performed on the DataFrame:

1. Text Standardization: - Categorical (text) columns: Department, Gender, Status, Location. Were cleaned by removing extra spaces and standardizing the uppercase/lowercase format. To avoid comparison errors between “HR” and “hr” or ” HR “.

2. Imputation of Missing Values: - Numeric variables (Performance Score or Age): null values were replaced with the median of each column. This is a best practice in data analysis that minimizes the impact of outliers. And maintains high dataset integrity, instead of deleting all records that have missing values. - Categorical variables: a label called “Not Specified” was created to still keep records of incomplete data and not shrink the dataset.

Creation of New Variables: -Tenure_Years: The years elapsed since the joining date are calculated. Up to the current date. This variable adds more interpretive capacity, to analyze how experience in the company impacts salary. -Performance_Cat: We took the Performance Score which had a value from 1-5 and converted it into a categorical variable assigning values such as (Low, Medium, High). This way it is easier to perform a comparative analysis by performance levels.

CHECKPOINT we now have a dataset without inconsistencies or null values. We are ready to perform an exploratory analysis and a statistical model.

5.2 Hypothesis testing: Salary Differnce by Gender

Code 
# Gender Data  (only Male y Female for t-test)
salary_male = df[df['Gender'] == 'Male']['Salary']
salary_female = df[df['Gender'] == 'Female']['Salary']

# t de Student Test (assuming variance is not the same)
t_stat, p_value = stats.ttest_ind(salary_male, salary_female, equal_var=False)

# Calculating difference and confidence interval , 95%
diff_mean = salary_male.mean() - salary_female.mean()
ci_male = stats.t.interval(0.95, len(salary_male)-1, loc=salary_male.mean(), scale=stats.sem(salary_male))
ci_female = stats.t.interval(0.95, len(salary_female)-1, loc=salary_female.mean(), scale=stats.sem(salary_female))

print("t-Student Test for different Avg:")
print(f"Male Avg Salary: ${salary_male.mean():.2f} (IC 95%: ${ci_male[0]:.2f} - ${ci_male[1]:.2f})")
print(f"Female Avg Salary: ${salary_female.mean():.2f} (IC 95%: ${ci_female[0]:.2f} - ${ci_female[1]:.2f})")
print(f"Difference: ${diff_mean:.2f}")
print(f"t Statistic: {t_stat:.4f}")
print(f"P-value: {p_value:.4f}")
print(f"¿Significant at 95%? {'Yes' if p_value < 0.05 else 'No'}")

5.2 T - Student Test:

“The observed salary difference between men ($6,090.86) and women ($5,793.08) is $297.78 (5.14%). However, the two-tailed t-test yields a p-value of 0.0934, which is greater than the significance threshold α=0.05. This means that we CANNOT reject the null hypothesis: there is not enough statistical evidence to claim that the gender pay gap is systematic in this organization at a 95% confidence level.”

5.4 Multiple Linear Regression Model:

“The multiple regression model (R²=0.008) explains only 0.8% of the salary variability, this indicates that the included variables (gender, department, experience, performance) have extremely low predictive power and do not explain the salary gap we want to identify. The coefficient for Gender_Male is $297.78 (p=0.093), confirming that it is not statistically significant, being male does not explain a salary gap that might exist. The IT and Sales departments do not show significant differences compared to HR (p>0.05). The most concerning finding is that Experience has a coefficient of only 1.21 (p=0.902), which confirms that tenure does not impact salary in this organization. This model suggests that unobserved factors (individual negotiation, manager biases, or errors in the compensation system) dominate salary determination. This analysis is crucial to be able to see what factors we should be taking into account when conducting an analysis with real data. It is a great exercise to challenge our preconceived ideas.”

6. Business Conclusions and Recommendations

Executive Summary

The analysis performed on our dataset reveals two key findings. First, there is no gender-based salary gap in this case. While this might be somewhat surprising, we can be confident that if the opposite were true, our robust analysis would have identified it. The second conclusion is that the compensation system is highly unusual (due to the synthetic nature of the dataset), and we observe no relationship between performance or experience and salary grade. In other words, in this analysis, employee performance and tenure do not serve to predict salary.

The insights presented help us think about how a dataset with this behavior impacts talent and the organization. We can answer problems that haven’t arrived yet, anticipate them. We have two insights that speak to talent. The current compensation system is not working because employees have no incentive to stay with the company. There is no clear path to earn more, and while this may not be the only reason people work, it is one of the main drivers. It is also important to highlight that employees categorized as high performers are at risk of turnover. This is where companies can lose a lot of money and key know-how that they don’t have in monetary terms, but which greatly impacts processes, organizational culture, and collective sentiment.

RECOMMENDATIONS

Short Term (0-3 months): - A complete audit of the current compensation system is needed, identifying which KPIs are driving raises and aligning everything more closely with performance.

Medium Term (3-12 months): - Redesign of internal salary grades. Establish a more robust salary scale that includes variables aligned with performance, business impact, tenure, career development, and key positions. Promote the design of a system based on clear expectations and measurable objectives.

Long Term (12+ months): - Work with Executive Leadership, Human Resources, and Compensation teams to implement a more transparent system aligned with business objectives and strategy.
